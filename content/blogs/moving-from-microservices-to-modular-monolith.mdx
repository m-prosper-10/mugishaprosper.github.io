At the beginning of the year, while planning the architecture for a new platform with a small team of three engineers, we made a decision that felt very mature on the whiteboard: we split the backend into four separate microservices.

We had an `auth-service`, a `core-api-service`, an `ai-inference-service`, and a `billing-notifications-service`—each in its own repository, with its own Docker image, its own database schema, and internal HTTP/gRPC calls between them.

Why did we do it? Because every conference talk from companies with 800 engineers says microservices give you independent scalability, clean domain isolation, and fault boundaries.

By July—six months in—our three-person team was spending more time managing the boundaries between our four services than actually building features for users.

In August, we spent a week merging three of those services back into a single **Modular Monolith**. Our deployment time dropped by 60%, our bug rate plummeted, and our velocity doubled overnight.

---

## The Microservice Tax When You Aren't Netflix

Nobody warns you how painful distributed systems overhead is when the same three people own all four services:

1. **One feature becomes four Pull Requests:** Adding a single field that flowed from user settings to the AI pipeline to billing required updating a shared TypeScript types package, publishing it, opening PRs across three repositories, and coordinating the exact order in which the three services deployed so nothing crashed in between.
2. **Function calls turn into network failures:** Inside a single process, calling `billingModule.checkQuota(userId)` takes `0.001ms` and never fails due to a DNS hiccup. Across microservices, that exact same check becomes a serialized HTTP call with timeouts, retries, auth headers, and partial failure states.
3. **Distributed transactions are a nightmare:** When creating an organization in `auth-service` succeeded, but initializing their workspace in `core-api-service` failed over the network, we had to write compensating rollback jobs just to keep two tables in sync—something a single PostgreSQL `BEGIN ... COMMIT` transaction gives you for free.

---

## The False Dichotomy: Spaghetti Monolith vs. Microservices

The reason people run away from monoliths is that they picture a 100,000-line ball of mud where every controller reaches directly into every database table across the entire app.

What clicked for us is that **you do not need a network boundary to enforce a module boundary.**

When we consolidated into a single repository and a single deployable service, we structured the codebase as a **Modular Monolith** with strict compile-time rules:

```text
src/modules/
├── identity/
│   ├── index.ts          # ONLY public facade exported to other modules
│   ├── internal/         # Private domain logic & DB queries
├── workspace/
│   ├── index.ts
│   ├── internal/
└── billing/
    ├── index.ts
    ├── internal/
```

We enforced two simple rules via ESLint (`eslint-plugin-boundaries`) and PostgreSQL schemas:
1. **No module is allowed to import from another module's `internal/` folder.** If `workspace` needs billing quota info, it can *only* call functions exported by `billing/index.ts`. If someone tries to import a billing database model directly inside `workspace`, the linter fails the build immediately.
2. **No cross-module SQL `JOIN`s** on internal tables. Each domain owns its tables exclusively.

---

## When Should You Actually Split a Service Out?

Notice I said we merged **three** of our four services back together. We kept the Python `ai-inference-service` separate for two concrete, physical reasons:
- It needed a completely different runtime stack and heavy ML/GPU dependencies (`PyTorch`, `ONNX`, document OCR libraries) that had no business bloating the main TypeScript API container.
- Its CPU and memory scaling profile was completely spiky compared to lightweight CRUD requests.

That became my new rule of thumb for systems architecture: **Keep everything in a well-structured modular monolith until a component has a genuinely different physical scaling requirement, hardware dependency, or security blast radius.**

If you ever *do* grow to fifty engineers and need to extract the `billing/` module into its own microservice later, having already enforced clean `index.ts` facade boundaries means extracting it takes an afternoon—instead of paying the distributed systems tax for two years before you even have the traffic to justify it.
